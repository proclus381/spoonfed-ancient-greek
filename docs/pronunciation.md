# How the deck's Greek sounds, and the other way to pronounce it

Nobody knows exactly how Plato sounded, so learners of Ancient Greek use one of a few pronunciations. This
deck's audio uses the **modern** one: each letter gets the sound it has in Greek today. Most courses outside
Greece use another one, based on how scholars reconstruct the Greek of Athens in the 5th century BC. This page
explains:
- both pronunciations;
- what each one means for you as a learner;
- why the deck uses the modern one;
- why you should know both.

**How the sounds are written here.**
- **Respellings** in italics, with the stressed syllable in capitals (*i-MIS*), are only a rough guide.
- **IPA in [square brackets]** gives the exact sound. IPA is the International Phonetic Alphabet:
  - ˈ comes before the stressed syllable: [iˈmis];
  - ː after a vowel or consonant means it is long: [ɛː];
  - in the reconstructed (Attic) transcriptions, ´ and ˆ over a vowel mark the pitch accent (see "Stress
    accent and pitch accent" below).
- **Comparisons with English, French or German words** are close but not exact unless they are marked
  "exactly". The difference is given where it matters.
- **More on IPA:** [Help:IPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA) (with audio for each symbol),
  [IPA chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Phonetic_Alphabet_chart),
  [Help:IPA/Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Greek).

## A few words first

- **Attic Greek.** The Greek of Athens in the 5th and 4th centuries BC: Plato, the tragedians. This is what
  *Athenaze* teaches.
- **Koine.** "Common" Greek: the Greek that spread around the Mediterranean after Alexander the Great, from
  about 300 BC. The New Testament is written in it. Its pronunciation was already moving towards today's.
- **Byzantine Greek.** The Greek of the Eastern Roman Empire (about AD 330–1453). Its pronunciation was close to
  the modern one.
- **Vowel length.** In Attic, a vowel could be *short* or *long* [ː]. A long vowel was held about twice as long,
  and the length could change the meaning or the grammatical form.
  - η and ω are always long; ε and ο are always short.
  - α, ι and υ can be either. Ordinary texts don't show which, but dictionaries do (ᾱ long, ᾰ short).
- **Diphthong.** Two vowel sounds glided together in one syllable, as in English *eye* or *boy*. In IPA the
  second part is marked ̯: [ai̯].
- **Aspiration** [ʰ]. A puff of breath after a consonant. Hold your hand in front of your mouth and say *pin*,
  then *spin*: the p of *pin* has a puff [pʰ], and the p of *spin* has none [p].
- **Stress accent and pitch accent.**
  - In English, the accented syllable is said louder and longer: *REcord* (the noun) and *reCORD* (the verb).
    That is a stress accent, and it is the accent of Greek today.
  - Attic had a **pitch** accent: the accented syllable was said on a higher pitch. It was not, as far as we
    know, necessarily louder. Think of it, only as an analogy, as a higher note in a tune.
  - In the reconstructed transcriptions here:
    - ´ means high pitch: [pʰílos]. On a long vowel or a diphthong, ´ means the pitch rises to its second half:
      [hɛ́ːlios].
    - ˆ, used only on long vowels and diphthongs, means high then falling: [dɔ̂ːron].
    - The grave (`) of the written accent was an acute toned down before another word. It gets no mark.
- **Itacism** (from *ita*, the modern name of η). The merging of several vowels and diphthongs into the single
  sound [i].
- **Erasmian pronunciation.** The school pronunciation used in most countries outside Greece. It goes back to
  Erasmus (1528), who argued that different letters must once have had different sounds. Every country has
  coloured it with its own language's sounds, so an Italian, a German and an English Erasmian sound
  different.
- **Reconstructed pronunciation.** A pronunciation that tries to match the Attic of the 5th century BC as
  closely as the evidence allows. The standard account is W. Sidney Allen's *Vox Graeca* (1987).

## 1. The modern pronunciation, applied to Ancient Greek

The spelling stays as it is: breathings, three accents, iota subscript. Only the sounds are today's.

**Five vowels, with no length difference.** Stressed vowels come out a little longer, but length never changes
the meaning. All five are pure: the tongue does not glide as it does in English *say*, *go* or *see*.

| IPA | Written | Sounds like | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| [i] | η, ι, υ, ει, οι, υι, ῃ | Italian or Spanish *i*. Roughly the *ee* of *see*, but short and with no glide. | ἡμεῖς [iˈmis] *i-MIS* "we" |
| [e] | ε, αι | Italian or Spanish *e*. It lies between the *e* of *bed* and the *ay* of *say*, and has no glide; *bed* is a fair approximation in many accents. | ἐγώ [eˈɣo] *e-GO* "I" |
| [o] | ο, ω, ῳ | Italian or Spanish *o*. It lies between the *o* of British *pot* and the *o* of *go*, and has no glide: not the English *go*. | λόγος [ˈloɣos] *LO-gos* "word" |
| [u] | ου | Italian or Spanish *u*. Roughly the *oo* of *food*, but short, with rounded lips and no glide. | οὐ [u] *u* "not" |
| [a] | α, ᾳ | Italian or Spanish *a*. It is an open central vowel, roughly the *a* of *father* but shorter: not the *a* of *cat*. | ἀγαθός [aɣaˈθos] *a-ga-THOS* "good" |

**The consonants that differ from English:**

| Written | IPA | Sounds like | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| β | [v] | English *v*, exactly | βίος [ˈvios] "life" |
| γ | [ɣ] | No English equivalent. Say [x] (see χ) with the voice on, like Spanish *g* in *lago*. | γάμος [ˈɣamos] "marriage" |
| γ before [e] or [i] | [ʝ] | Close to the *y* of *yes*, but with audible friction | γῆ [ʝi] "earth" |
| δ | [ð] | English *th* in *this*, exactly | δῶρον [ˈðoron] "gift" |
| θ | [θ] | English *th* in *thin*, exactly | θεός [θeˈos] "god" |
| φ | [f] | English *f*, exactly | φίλος [ˈfilos] "friend" |
| χ | [x] | German *ch* in *Bach*, or Scottish *loch* | χώρα [ˈxora] "land" |
| χ before [e] or [i] | [ç] | German *ch* in *ich*. English has no such sound; the closest is the *h* of *huge* for some speakers. | χείρ [çir] "hand" |
| ζ | [z] | English *z* in *zoo* | ζυγόν [ziˈɣon] "yoke" |
| ρ | [r] | A tapped *r*, a short trill in clusters, like Spanish or Italian *r*. Not English *r*. | ῥήτωρ [ˈritor] "speaker" |
| π, τ, κ | [p t k] | *p t k* with no puff of breath, as in *spot, stop, Scot*, or as in French or Spanish. English speakers tend to add the puff. | πέτρα [ˈpetra] "rock" |

- **αυ, ευ, ηυ** are [av ev iv] before a vowel or a voiced consonant. Before π τ κ φ θ χ σ ξ ψ they are
  [af ef if]. So αὐτός is [afˈtos] *af-TOS* "self", and εὖ is [ef] "well".
- **γγ and γκ** are [ŋɡ], *ng* as in *finger*: ἔγκλημα [ˈeŋɡlima] "charge". Before [e] or [i] the two
  sounds shift towards the *y* position, [ɲɟ]: ἄγγελος [ˈaɲɟelos] "messenger".
- **μπ and ντ** inside a word are [mb] and [nd]: πέντε [ˈpende] "five".
- **σ** is [z] before a voiced consonant: κόσμος [ˈkozmos] "order".
- **No [h]:** the rough breathing (ἁ) is silent, so ὅρος "boundary" and ὄρος "mountain" are both [ˈoros].
- **Double consonants** are said as single ones: ἄλλος [ˈalos] "other".
- **The accent is stress.** The syllable with an accent mark is stressed, and all three marks sound the same.

**A deck sentence:** Φλέγει ὁ ἥλιος. "The sun is blazing." [ˈfleʝi o ˈilios] *FLE-yi o I-li-os*

## 2. Allen's reconstruction, compared

| Written | Modern | Reconstructed (Attic, 5th c. BC) | Sounds like (reconstructed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| η | [i] | [ɛː] | A long open *e*. Close to the vowel of *air* in non-rhotic British English (no r), or French *è* in *fête* held long. |
| ει | [i] | [eː] | German *ee* in *See*, which has no glide: not English *say*. |
| ω | [o] | [ɔː] | Roughly British *aw* in *law* or *thought*. |
| ου | [u] | [uː] (earlier [oː]) | German *u* in *Schuh*, roughly *oo* in *food* but with no glide. The earlier [oː] is German *o* in *so*: not English *go*. |
| υ | [i] | [y], long [yː] | French *u* in *tu*, German *ü*: say [i] with rounded lips. No English equivalent. |
| ε, ο | [e], [o] | [e], [o] (short) | As in modern Greek. |
| α, ι | [a], [i] | [a] or [aː], [i] or [iː] | The same vowels, short or long. |
| αι | [e] | [ai̯] | Roughly English *eye*. |
| οι | [i] | [oi̯] | Roughly English *boy*. |
| αυ | [av], [af] | [au̯] | Roughly English *how*. |
| ευ | [ev], [ef] | [eu̯] | [e] gliding to [u] in one syllable. No English equivalent: not *you* or *feud*. |
| ᾳ, ῃ, ῳ | [a], [i], [o] | [aːi̯], [ɛːi̯], [ɔːi̯] | The long vowel with a short [i] glide after it. Many courses drop the glide. |
| β, γ, δ | [v], [ɣ]/[ʝ], [ð] | [b], [ɡ], [d] | English *b*, *g* in *go*, *d* |
| γ before κ γ χ ξ | [ŋ] | [ŋ] | *ng* in *sing*: ἄγγελος [áŋɡelos] |
| φ, θ, χ | [f], [θ], [x]/[ç] | [pʰ], [tʰ], [kʰ] | *p t k* with a puff of breath, like the start of English *pot, top, cot*. Not [f θ x]. |
| π, τ, κ | [p t k] | [p t k] | With no puff, as in *spot, stop, Scot*. English speakers must take care here, or π τ κ will sound like φ θ χ. |
| ζ | [z] | [zd] | *sd* in *wisdom* |
| rough breathing | silent | [h] | *h* in *hat* |
| ρ | [r] | [r]; at the start of a word, voiceless [r̥] | A trilled *r*, like Italian *r*; ῥ at the start of a word is breathy |
| σ before a voiced consonant | [z] | [z] | κόσμος [kózmos] |
| double consonants | single | long: λλ [lː], ττ [tː] | Held longer, like the *k–c* in *bookcase* (one long k) or Italian *notte*: ἄλλος [álːos] |
| accent | stress, ˈ | pitch, ´ ˆ | See "Stress accent and pitch accent" above |

**The same sentence:** Φλέγει ὁ ἥλιος. [pʰléɡeː ho hɛ́ːlios]

Some caveats:
- **Attic itself was changing.** ου had become [uː] by the 4th century BC, ζ became [z] from about 350 BC, and
  ει became [iː] from the late 4th century.
- **Courses simplify.** Few pronounce the pitch accent. Many use [f] and [θ] for φ and θ, because English
  speakers confuse the aspirated and plain stops. Even "reconstructed" courses differ in which period they aim
  at.

## 3. What the choice means for you

### What the modern pronunciation makes harder

- **Words and endings that sound the same:**

  | Forms | Modern | Reconstructed |
  |---|---|---|
  | ἡμεῖς "we" / ὑμεῖς "you" | both [iˈmis] | [hɛːmêːs] / [hyːmêːs] |
  | λύει "he loosens" / λύῃ "he may loosen" / λύοι "he might loosen" | all [ˈlii] | [lýːeː] / [lýːɛːi̯] / [lýːoi̯] |
  | λύομεν "we loosen" / λύωμεν "let us loosen" | both [ˈliomen] | [lýːomen] / [lýːɔːmen] |
  | λύεσθε "you are loosened" / λύεσθαι "to be loosened" | both [ˈliesθe] | [lýːestʰe] / [lýːestʰai̯] |

  In a sentence, the context usually tells you which one it is. When you write, as on the cloze cards, the
  sound won't tell you the spelling: you have to know it.
- **Some grammar can't be heard.** It has to be learnt from the page:
  - *The augment* is the mark of a past tense at the start of a verb. It is either an added ε- (λύω → ἔλυον)
    or a lengthened first vowel.
    - Reconstructed: ἄγω [áɡɔː] "I lead" → ἦγον [ɛ̂ːɡon] "I was leading". You hear the vowel made long.
    - Modern: [ˈaɣo] → [ˈiɣon]. The link is lost.
  - *Contraction* is two vowels meeting inside a word and fusing: φιλέω [pʰiléɔː] → φιλῶ [pʰilɔ̂ː]. The results
    (ε + ε → ει [eː], ο + ο → ου [oː], ε + α → η [ɛː]) make sense with the old sounds, not the new ones.
  - *Aspiration* explains spellings: κατά + ἡμέραν → καθ' ἡμέραν [katʰɛːméraːn]. The τ [t] runs into the [h]
    and becomes θ [tʰ]. In modern sound, [kaθ iˈmeran], it is just a rule to memorise.
- **The accent rules depend on vowel length.** Where an accent can stand, and whether it is acute or
  circumflex, depends on which vowels are long:
  - ἄνθρωπος [ántʰrɔːpos] but ἀνθρώπου [antʰrɔ́ːpuː];
  - δῶρον [dɔ̂ːron] but δώρου [dɔ́ːruː].

  In the modern pronunciation these are [ˈanθropos] / [anˈθropu] and [ˈðoron] / [ˈðoru], with no length. So
  you learn the rules from the page, not by ear.
- **Verse rhythm.** Greek poetry (Homer, the tragedies) is built from patterns of long and short syllables,
  not stressed and unstressed ones as in English. That pattern is the *metre*. Without length you can work out
  a line's rhythm on paper, but you can't hear it.

### What the modern pronunciation makes easier

- **Natural audio.** It is a living pronunciation, so computer voices trained on native speakers read it
  fluently, with real rhythm and intonation. Recordings by fluent readers are easy to find; for example, the
  Orthodox Church reads the New Testament aloud in it. No voice of that quality exists for reconstructed
  Attic.
- **One standard.** The modern pronunciation is the same everywhere. Erasmian varies by country and by
  teacher.
- **Easier to say.** There are five vowels with no length, no difference between [p] and [pʰ], and no pitch. You
  can read aloud fluently early on and spend your attention on the grammar.
- **Closer to later texts.** Much of the modern system had arrived by Koine times, and almost all of it by
  Byzantine times. For the New Testament and Byzantine authors, the modern pronunciation is closer to how
  their writers spoke than 5th-century Attic is.

### Who uses what

- **Schools and universities outside Greece** mostly use Erasmian, including in Italy, Germany, Spain and the
  US.
- **British schools** use a reconstruction based on Allen.
- **Greece and Orthodox theology faculties** read Greek of every period with the modern pronunciation.
- **Spoken-Greek courses** differ:
  - Ioannis Stratakis, whose recordings of classical texts are at ancientgreek.eu, uses his own reconstruction
    of the ancient pronunciation, based on what ancient writers say about how Greek sounded.
  - The Polis Institute in Jerusalem uses one close to Erasmian.
  - Randall Buth uses a reconstructed Koine.

## 4. Why I chose the modern pronunciation, and why you should learn both

**Why I chose it.**
- **The listening card is half the deck.** You are meant to understand a sentence from its sound alone.
  That only works if the audio sounds like real speech.
- **Voicing 4,735 sentences meant using a computer voice.** The good ones read Greek only in the modern
  pronunciation, fluently and at natural speed, and the same from the first card to the last. Synthetic
  voices for a reconstructed pronunciation sound robotic.
- **I preferred natural speech in a later pronunciation to unnatural speech in an earlier one.** The modern
  pronunciation is not how Plato sounded, and the deck does not claim it is. Erasmian isn't either.

**Why you should still learn the reconstructed one.**
- It makes spelling, endings, augments and contractions make sense.
- The accent rules are about vowel length.
- Verse can't be heard without it.
- Most teachers and textbooks outside Greece use Erasmian or a reconstruction, and so do most recordings of
  ancient texts. You should understand them and be understood.
- Grammars and commentaries describe sounds, length and metre in its terms.

**In practice.**
- **Learn the table in §2.** It takes an afternoon.
- **Train your ear with the deck.** When the audio gives you [i], ask yourself which of η ι υ ει οι it is.
  The cloze cards make you answer.
- **Use the reconstructed sounds for verse and accents.** When you read verse, or an accent puzzles you,
  think in the reconstructed sounds.
- **Hear the reconstructed one spoken.** Ioannis Stratakis has recorded the main Greek text of each chapter of
  *Athenaze* I in a reconstructed pronunciation, free on request:
  [ancientgreek.eu](https://ancientgreek.eu/edu/athenaze-1.html). The deck follows the same chapters.
- **Choose one pronunciation for reading aloud** and keep to it. Listening to both, the deck and Stratakis's
  recording, does no harm.
